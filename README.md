# First-Principles-Simulation-of-2D-Quantum-Systems
This project presents a first-principles investigation into the electronic properties and behavior of two-dimensional (2D) quantum systems, specifically focusing on the Hydrogen atom, the Helium atom, and the Quantum Harmonic Oscillator.   The exploration of matter in reduced dimensions is critical for understanding modern 2D materials (such as graphene and transition metal dichalcogenides) and excitons in semiconductors. Confinement to a plane fundamentally alters electron behavior, modifying the nature of quantum states and enhancing electron-electron interactions. For example, while the Coulomb interaction scales as $1/r$ in three dimensions, the effective potential in two dimensions typically behaves as $ln(r)$ or $1/r$, resulting in stronger binding energies and shifted scaling laws. This project models these foundational shifts, providing reliable conceptual benchmarks for theoretical frameworks.

**Methodology & Key Features

The computational approach centers on grid-based numerical simulations utilizing the efficient split-operator Fourier transform technique on a real-space grid.   Imaginary Time Evolution (ITE): Implemented to determine the quantum ground states of the systems, with the 2D Quantum Harmonic Oscillator serving as a visual demonstration of the ITE convergence process.   2D Hydrogen & The Stark Effect: Accurately reproduces the spherically symmetric ground state. The simulations quantitatively analyze the weak-field Stark effect, yielding a static dipole polarizability of 0.1940 a.u., and qualitatively demonstrate field ionization via quantum tunnelling in strong fields.   2D Helium & Electron Correlation: Applies the Hartree Self-Consistent Field (SCF) approximation to handle electron-electron interactions. The model successfully converges to a self-consistent solution that demonstrates electron polarization in external fields (yielding a static dipole polarizability of 0.1151 a.u.). Furthermore, it calculates the correlation energy (-4.352920 a.u.) to highlight the critical contribution of instantaneous electron interactions that fall outside mean-field limitations.
